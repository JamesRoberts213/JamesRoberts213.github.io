Mitigating Hand-Arm Vibration Syndrome (HAVS)

My individual responsibility in Group 5 at Lancaster University was the mathematical analysis. I derived the coupled 2-DOF equations of motion, calculated and interpreted the natural frequencies and modal damping, and analysed transmissibility. This work provided the mathematical basis the group used to assess its damping concept.

  • Decoupled Floating Handle ($m_2$): Isolates the user's grip from the reciprocating saw body ($m_1$) using dual passive springs ($k_{\text{Handle}} = 7100\text{ N/m}$) on linear guide rails.
  • PID-Controlled Lorentz Actuator ($F = BIL$): Generates a contactless counter-force between $m_1$ and $m_2$, using the heavier saw body as a third-law vibration sink.

Mathematical Foundation & Analytical Derivations

Core analytical framework derived to model the decoupled saw body ($m_1 = 2.8\text{ kg}$) and handle ($m_2 = 1.2\text{ kg}$) across varying cutting substrates and damping regimes.

Applying Newton's Second and Third Laws to the Free Body Diagrams of the main saw body ($x_1$) and isolated handle ($x_2$), subject to motor excitation $F_{\text{Saw}}(t) = 412.68\sin(293.22t)$ and PID-controlled Lorentz force $F_{\text{Lorentz}}$:

Coupled Second-Order Differential Equations
$$m_1 \ddot{x}_1 + k_{\text{Board}} x_1 + c_{\text{Board}} \dot{x}_1 + k_{\text{Handle}}(x_1 - x_2) + c_{\text{Handle}}(\dot{x}_1 - \dot{x}_2) = F_0 \sin(\omega t) - F_{\text{Lorentz}}$$ $$m_2 \ddot{x}_2 + k_{\text{Arm}} x_2 + c_{\text{Arm}} \dot{x}_2 - k_{\text{Handle}}(x_1 - x_2) - c_{\text{Handle}}(\dot{x}_1 - \dot{x}_2) = F_{\text{Lorentz}}$$
Matrix State Formulation $[M]\{\ddot{x}\} + [C]\{\dot{x}\} + [K]\{x\} = \{F\}$
$$\begin{bmatrix} m_1 & 0 \\ 0 & m_2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{Bmatrix} \ddot{x}_1 \\ \ddot{x}_2 \end{Bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} c_{\text{Board}} + c_{\text{Handle}} & -c_{\text{Handle}} \\ -c_{\text{Handle}} & c_{\text{Arm}} + c_{\text{Handle}} \end{bmatrix} \begin{Bmatrix} \dot{x}_1 \\ \dot{x}_2 \end{Bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} k_{\text{Board}} + k_{\text{Handle}} & -k_{\text{Handle}} \\ -k_{\text{Handle}} & k_{\text{Arm}} + k_{\text{Handle}} \end{bmatrix} \begin{Bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{Bmatrix} = \begin{Bmatrix} F_0\sin(\omega t) - F_{\text{Lorentz}} \\ F_{\text{Lorentz}} \end{Bmatrix}$$

Setting damping and external forcing to zero and solving the characteristic determinant $\det([K] - \omega^2 [M]) = 0$ with $\lambda = \omega^2$:

Characteristic Eigenvalue Polynomial
$$3.36\lambda^2 - 1.0764\times 10^6\lambda + 1.4606\times 10^{10} = 0 \implies \begin{cases} \lambda_1 = 14{,}160 \implies \omega_1 = 119\text{ rad/s} \quad (f_1 = 18.9\text{ Hz}) \\ \lambda_2 = 305{,}600 \implies \omega_2 = 553\text{ rad/s} \quad (f_2 = 88.0\text{ Hz}) \end{cases}$$

Because the Makita DJR186 operates at $2800\text{ SPM}$ ($f = 46.67\text{ Hz}$), the excitation frequency sits cleanly in the isolation valley between $f_1 = 18.9\text{ Hz}$ and $f_2 = 88.0\text{ Hz}$, avoiding resonance. Evaluating $k_{\text{Board}} = EA/L$ across multiple workpiece materials proved that the lower modal frequency remains invariant at $\approx 19.0\text{ Hz}$ regardless of cut material:

Using eigenvectors $\varphi_1 = \{1,\; 115.13\}^T$ and $\varphi_2 = \{1,\; 0.200\}^T$, the modal damping ratios $\zeta_i$ and damped natural frequencies $\omega_{d,i}$ were derived via Rayleigh-type modal projection:

Modal Damping Ratios & Passive Transmissibility
$$\zeta_i = \frac{\varphi_i^T [C] \varphi_i}{2\omega_i \varphi_i^T [M] \varphi_i} \implies \begin{cases} \zeta_1 = 0.44 \;\;(\text{Under-damped, } \omega_{d,1} = 106.86\text{ rad/s}) \\ \zeta_2 = 1.14 \;\;(\text{Over-damped, exponential decay}) \end{cases}$$ $$T_1 = \sqrt{\frac{1 + (2\zeta_1 r_1)^2}{(1 - r_1^2)^2 + (2\zeta_1 r_1)^2}} = 0.43 \quad \left(r_1 = \frac{293.22}{119} = 2.46\right)$$

Passive isolation alone yields a transmissibility of $T_1 = 0.43$ ($\approx 40\%$ amplitude reduction in Simulink). Adding the closed-loop PID Lorentz force actuator further suppresses handle acceleration by $74\%$ even when realistically saturated at $\pm 5\text{ N}$.

Key Engineering Takeaways & Retrospective

Third-Law Vibration Sink Trade-Off

Decoupling the handle intentionally increases acceleration in the heavier saw body ($m_1$) so it absorbs oscillatory energy, protecting the operator's hand ($m_2$).

Real-World Actuator Saturation

While an ideal unsaturated PID requires $\pm 11\text{ N}$ for near-zero handle motion, capping output at a realistic $\pm 5\text{ N}$ ($F=BIL$) still achieves a $74\%$ vibration reduction.

Hybrid Passive + Active Synergy

Passive helical springs deliver $\sim 40\%$ attenuation with zero electrical draw, drastically lowering the continuous current and thermal load on the active Lorentz coil.

Substrate-Invariant Lower Mode

Eigenvalue sweeps across Acrylic, Oak, and Stainless Steel confirmed that the first natural frequency remains locked at $\approx 19.0\text{ Hz}$, ensuring predictable isolation across jobsite materials.

Phase Latency & Sensor Noise

Ideal Simulink models assume zero feedback delay at $46.7\text{ Hz}$; physical prototypes must account for accelerometer noise, coil heating, and phase-lag compensation.

Regulatory HAVS Compliance

The report uses the UK 2.5 m/s² Exposure Action Value as context. A reduction in daily exposure would need to be confirmed with physical measurements of the tool in use.

Group Design Concept & Selection

The team selected a hybrid damping concept: passive springs isolate the handle without drawing electrical power, while an active Lorentz-force actuator provides additional control. The report frames the combination as a way to reduce the load on the active system while retaining a compact handheld tool.

Design decision matrix from the report

The report notes that friction systems may need frequent replacement, viscous systems depend on seal integrity, and a counterweight would affect how the saw handles. The hybrid spring and Lorentz concept was chosen to balance isolation, control and practical use. A design trade-off is that damping can also reduce some force delivered to the cut.

Detailed System Design

The group represented the saw as a coupled two-mass system. The handle is allowed to move relative to the saw body, with spring and damping elements between them; the active actuator applies an equal-and-opposite Lorentz force.

SubsystemRole in the conceptDesign consideration
Saw body, m₁Main tool mass and reaction body for the actuatorThe report treats increased body motion as a third-law trade-off that can reduce motion at the handle.
Floating handle, m₂Separates the user's grip from the reciprocating bodyConnected with a handle spring and passive damping; the model also includes arm stiffness and damping.
Active actuatorApplies a counter-force between the two massesUses the Lorentz relation F = BIL; the report evaluates a practical ±5 N saturation case.

The report cautions that the ideal controller assumes effectively perfect phase alignment. It does not model sensor noise, actuator heating or feedback latency. The group recommends testing those effects in a physical prototype before making product performance claims.

Commercialisation Potential

Opportunity

The report identifies a possible consumer-market gap for lower-vibration power tools. A lower vibration rating could appeal to users who want to reduce exposure and could support adapting the concept to other tools if the design is validated.

Costs and constraints

The added components may increase price, power consumption, maintenance and wear, and may reduce cutting effectiveness. The report estimates about £10,000 for initial proof-of-concept prototyping, precision equipment and labour, before manufacturing and distribution costs.

Evidence needed before launch

  • Prototype testing: compare measured vibration with the standard saw in realistic cutting conditions.
  • Controller validation: assess sensor noise, phase lag, actuator temperature and battery demand.
  • Durability review: investigate wear, service intervals, reliability and the effect on tool performance.
  • Safety claims: substantiate any claim that reduced vibration lowers HAVS risk with suitable evidence.

Group SWOT Analysis

Strengths

  • Power tools are widely used and produce vibration that the concept aims to reduce.
  • Combines passive isolation with active control.
  • Could offer an alternative for users concerned about vibration exposure.

Weaknesses

  • Added components may raise the purchase price.
  • Damping may reduce cutting effectiveness.
  • Additional parts may increase maintenance and affect battery life.

Opportunities

  • Adapt the system to other reciprocating saws and power tools.
  • Reach consumers seeking lower-vibration tools and protective equipment markets.

Threats

  • Competitors may introduce alternative damping systems.
  • Changes in safety requirements could require design updates.

Group Conclusions & Recommendations

The group concluded that its hybrid concept could reduce handle vibration in the model, with the saturated ±5 N case giving a reported reduction of about 74% from the baseline and passive isolation alone giving about 40%. The report sees potential for a safer-feeling tool and adaptation to other products, while recognising higher cost, power use and reduced cutting performance as trade-offs. These are simulation findings; they do not establish a measured reduction in user injury risk.

Recommended next steps

  • Build and physically test a prototype against the original saw.
  • Measure vibration during representative cutting tasks and compare it with the model.
  • Evaluate continuous operation, heating and battery-life effects.
  • Test control latency and tune the PID for realistic sensor and actuator response.

Validation before commercial claims

Confirm performance and durability across materials and use conditions, then assess exposure implications using appropriate measurements. The report identifies these checks as future work rather than completed testing.
